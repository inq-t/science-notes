# Bell's Theorem Revisited: Structural Collapse from Entropy Geometry

Bell's theorem is widely cited as definitive evidence that the physical world is forced to choose: either locality must be abandoned, or realism must yield. Yet this dilemma conceals a deeper structural assumption—one rarely made explicit. The standard argument presupposes a flat geometry of resolution: it assumes that the space of possibilities is already perfectly divided, every alternative sharply distinguishable, and all correlations defined on an informationally uniform background. The Total Entropic Quantity (TEQ) framework overturns this unexamined starting point. In TEQ, locality, realism, and even the act of measurement itself arise from the curved geometry of entropy-weighted resolution, not as metaphysical postulates but as structural consequences. Distinguishability is not given, but selected: it is a product of entropy geometry, and can be sharply limited or curved. This paper revisits Bell's inequality in this new light. Collapse is reframed—not as a discontinuous event or an observer-induced mystery, but as the stabilization of resolution under entropy curvature. We show that the statistical structure of Bell-type experiments is not a paradox, but the signature of curved entropy geometry selecting resolution-stable paths. No hidden variables, no special role for the observer, and no violation of empirical predictions are required. Instead, what appears as "nonlocality" is reinterpreted as evidence for shared entropy curvature; what appears as a quantum mystery becomes a geometric fact. By deriving Bell-type violations from first principles within TEQ, we preserve all experimental results while providing a unified, falsifiable, and conceptually grounded explanation. The flatness assumption at the heart of the standard reading is not denied—it is generalized, allowing the theory to recover known physics as a limit, while predicting possible deviations in regimes of extreme entropy curvature. This approach reframes one of the deepest puzzles of quantum theory as a question of geometry, not ontology.
